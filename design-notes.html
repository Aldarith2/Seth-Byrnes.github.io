Development Philosophy and Practical Lessons

Consider every team member's feedback, but centralize decision-making power in the producer or a lead designer. No game is built by committee.

Simplified systems get delivered fastest and with fewer iterations, and this costs less.

Games only need to be complex enough for the fun they want to deliver. Complexity is not fun on it's own, and this costs more to develop and troubleshoot.

Not every game gets a sequel, but sequels are often the best return on investment for a studio. They are a chance to refine the original featureset to perfection.

Try designing the manual before you design the game. If the instructions are easy to understand, the functionality will be easier to design clearly.

Players expect the game to do what it says on the box. Do not promise anything your game does not deliver.

Control subjects are usually too nice in QA tests, but your boss is often succinct.

Do not wait until the end to design sound, but do wait until the last of the main functionality exists before going beyond placeholders.

The vibe is everything. Stylized art direction is more valuable to the feeling than fidelity, the rest can be trusted to mechanics and audio.

Team leads must study the demographic. If a player does not like the vibe, they will not invest money or mental facility.

Somebody at market wants your game, but the budget needs to match.

To maintain good reviews on launch, games need more style than fidelity, more charm than dialogue, and fewer bugs than shipped features.
